Why a good roster takes more than one score

What the numbers describe, and what they leave out. Roster evaluation through an illustrative example.

In this article

Same average, different experienceRequirements and objectivesThe choices behind a scoreInterpreting optimalityResults people can review

Evaluating an automatically generated roster raises several questions. Are the required staff assigned? How many requests were accommodated? Is the workload concentrated on particular people? Can the team review, adjust, and finalize the result?

These questions address different aspects of quality. To judge a roster, we need to understand what each measure describes and what it can leave out.

Same average, different experience

Consider four nurses with the same eligibility for night shifts and the same contracted workload. The assignment counts below are a fictional example.

Both rosters have the same average. Looking at individual assignments reveals a different distribution of work. An average helps describe the overall workload, but it does not show the differences each person experiences.

Equal counts are not a universal answer either. In practice, availability, personal preferences, and assignments in earlier periods can differ. Judging whether a difference is reasonable requires understanding the circumstances behind the numbers.

Requirements and objectives

When discussing roster quality, we first need to distinguish requirements that must be satisfied from objectives we want to improve where possible. Scheduling problems commonly combine staffing requirements with objectives such as accommodating employee requests. Google’s public OR-Tools example illustrates this distinction.

When several rosters meet the requirements, choosing between them involves trade-offs. Accommodating more requests may concentrate work in a particular period. Preserving assignments that have already been announced may leave less room for other improvements.

The choices behind a score

An aggregate score is useful for comparing candidates. It also reflects choices about which factors matter and how much importance each receives.

To interpret an improved score, we need to understand what improved and which burdens increased along the way.

Even when two results use the same evaluation method, their totals alone cannot explain the change. If the overall request fulfillment rate increases, examining which requests were accommodated and who needed adjustments provides information for the next decision.

Interpreting optimality

The word “optimal” needs the same context. In optimization, optimality is assessed against a defined model and objective. A condition that matters in practice cannot be assumed satisfied simply because the computed solution is optimal, if that condition was never included in the model.

Finding a feasible solution within a time limit is also distinct from establishing optimality. OR-Tools reports these outcomes separately. An explanation of a result should include the conditions used in the calculation and what was actually established.

Results people can review

The person reviewing a roster needs information that makes these differences understandable: the requirements applied, the distribution of work, requests that could not be accommodated, and changes from the previous version. This gives them a basis for accepting or revising the result.

The same principle applies when presenting automation performance. Generation time or request fulfillment rates need context about the problem size and the conditions used. Changing the comparison conditions changes what the numbers mean.

ORD Systems develops systems that connect operational requirements expressed in language with mathematical optimization. RN-AI applies this approach to nurse scheduling.

We place importance on helping people review results and make the next decision. An explanation of a good roster should make clear what it satisfies, which trade-offs it involves, and what still needs to be checked.
